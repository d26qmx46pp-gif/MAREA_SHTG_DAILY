# Acromegaly Update — Monday August 31

**Bottom line:** It's a quiet week for acromegaly-specific research — no top-tier trials, just a handful of diagnostic and mechanistic papers — while rare disease industry news brought two new FDA approvals (Priovant's Lisraya for dermatomyositis and Takeda/Protagonist's Mimrylo for polycythemia vera) that show regulators keep clearing novel mechanisms for small patient populations.

## Part A — Acromegaly research

*Note: the standard top-tier-journal search returned zero hits this week; the query below was broadened to drop the journal-tier filter, so these papers run in specialty and case-report venues rather than NEJM/Lancet/JAMA-tier journals.*

**Thyroid abnormalities in acromegaly in the era of improved disease control: a two-stage study**
Diniz Leite, da Rocha, Diniz Leite | Journal of Endocrinological Investigation | [PMID: 42171673](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42171673/) | DOI: 10.1089/thy.2023.0141
This Brazilian study followed 92 acromegaly patients over 28 years, tracking nearly 1,000 IGF-1 measurements alongside thyroid ultrasounds in 74 of them. Most patients (70%) had their disease under control, and four had thyroid cancer — but three of those four were in patients whose acromegaly was already well managed, suggesting good biochemical control doesn't fully protect the thyroid. Two-thirds of patients had thyroid nodules and over a quarter had diffuse thyroid enlargement, though these didn't cluster neatly by disease activity. The takeaway: even with modern treatment bringing growth hormone under control, clinicians should keep watching the thyroid, since goiter and nodules remain common and cancer risk isn't fully eliminated.

**Quantitative analysis and clinical evaluation of 20- and 22-kDa growth hormone isoforms in somatotroph adenoma using immunocapture coupled with LC-MS/MS**
Hu, Chen, Xia | Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine | [PMID: 42154802](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42154802/) | DOI: 10.1007/s12020-024-04082-x
Growth hormone circulates in two forms — a 22-kDa and a 20-kDa isoform — and separating them with standard immunoassays is notoriously imprecise. This group built a mass-spectrometry-based method that cleanly measures both, tested it on serum from 63 acromegaly patients, and found it more accurate than conventional immunoassay, though it read systematically lower total GH levels. A more precise handle on GH isoforms could sharpen how doctors judge whether a patient's acromegaly is truly in biochemical remission, which currently hinges on exactly these measurements.

**Transcriptional profiling of growth hormone-secreting pituitary adenoma stem-like cells: unveiling the significant role of CXCR4 in tumorigenesis and clinical applications**
Yuan, Yang, Li | MedComm | [PMID: 42639247](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42639247/) | DOI: 10.1002/mco2.70946
Researchers isolated stem-like cells from GH-secreting pituitary tumors and ran RNA sequencing to map out which genes drive their growth. They zeroed in on CXCR4, a chemokine receptor, and showed it plays a meaningful role in how these tumor stem cells behave and proliferate. It's early, mechanistic work, but it points to CXCR4 as a potential new drug target for the subset of acromegaly cases driven by more aggressive, treatment-resistant pituitary tumors.

**Delayed diagnosis of acromegaly when lower facial features were obscured by masks during the COVID-19 pandemic**
Sakaue, Mukai, Kajimoto | JCEM Case Reports | [PMID: 42666735](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42666735/) | DOI: 10.1210/jcemcr/luag163
A case report worth flagging for clinical awareness: a 74-year-old woman with poorly controlled diabetes and unusual insulin resistance wasn't diagnosed with acromegaly until doctors got a good look at her unmasked face and spotted the classic coarsened features. The authors argue that mask-wearing during COVID-19 may have quietly delayed acromegaly diagnoses more broadly, since facial changes are often the first clue that prompts a workup — a reminder to look carefully at the whole face, not just labs, in patients with treatment-resistant diabetes or hypertension.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**FDA approves first oral drug for dermatomyositis: Priovant's Lisraya**
BioPharma Dive, published Aug. 28, 2026 | https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/fda-priovant-pfizer-lisraya-brepocitinib-approved/829021/
The FDA cleared brepocitinib (brand name Lisraya), a once-daily pill from Priovant Therapeutics — a Roivant Sciences subsidiary — for dermatomyositis, a rare autoimmune disease causing muscle weakness and skin lesions that affects an estimated 34,000 people in the U.S. The drug, originally discovered by Pfizer, blocks two JAK-family proteins (TYK2 and JAK1). In Phase 3 testing it improved muscle strength and skin symptoms and let many patients cut back on steroids; a companion JAMA Dermatology paper published the same week showed about three-quarters of patients had meaningful itch improvement after a year, versus a third on placebo. It carries the same boxed warning as other JAK inhibitors, and a 30-day supply will list at $35,000.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* It's a case study in a "hub-and-spoke" biotech (Roivant) taking a licensed asset from a pharma giant, threading it through a narrow, competition-free rare-disease indication, and reaching approval and commercial launch on its own — a playbook other subsidiary biotechs are watching closely.

**FDA approves Takeda and Protagonist's Mimrylo for a rare blood cancer**
STAT News, published Aug. 30, 2026 | https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/30/fda-approves-mimrylo-takeda-protagonist-treatment-for-polycythemia-vera/
The FDA approved rusfertide (brand name Mimrylo), a weekly injectable developed by Protagonist Therapeutics and licensed to Takeda, for polycythemia vera — a rare, slow-growing blood cancer in which the body overproduces red blood cells, thickening the blood and raising the risk of heart attack, stroke, and clots. Rusfertide is a hepcidin-mimetic, working through a different mechanism than existing PV therapies, and in the pivotal Phase 3 trial it showed roughly a 77% response rate, with many patients able to reduce or stop the frequent therapeutic phlebotomies (blood draws) that are the current mainstay of PV management. Takeda originally licensed the drug from Protagonist for $300 million upfront in 2024, with Protagonist since opting for additional fixed payouts of several hundred million dollars rather than splitting future U.S. profits.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* It's a validating data point for hepcidin-pathway drugs generally, and for the licensing model where a large pharma bankrolls a smaller biotech's rare-disease asset through Phase 3 and regulatory review in exchange for future royalties or a buyout of profit-share rights.

## What to watch

Both halves of this week's digest point the same direction: regulators are still willing to clear genuinely novel mechanisms — a JAK inhibitor repositioned into a narrow skin/muscle disease, a hepcidin mimetic for a blood disorder — when the unmet need is high and the competition is thin, exactly the kind of opening acromegaly-focused developers have long exploited with drugs like pasireotide and paltusotine. On the acromegaly side itself, this week's papers were more about sharpening diagnosis and monitoring (better GH isoform assays, a reminder not to let masks or reflex lab-chasing delay recognition) than about new therapies — worth watching whether any of the mechanistic CXCR4 work in pituitary tumor stem cells eventually feeds into a treatment-resistant-disease program.
