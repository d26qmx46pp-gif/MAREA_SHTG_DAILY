# Acromegaly Update — Monday June 1

**Bottom line:** New Pituitary research this week highlights the cardiac and bone risks that persist even in treated acromegaly patients, while the rare disease industry grapples with a mixed regulatory environment — one notable ASCO data release and continued fallout from FDA leadership turnover.

---

## Part A — Acromegaly Research

**Speckle-tracking echocardiography reveals the synergistic impact of GH/IGF-1 excess and metabolic dysregulation on cardiac dysfunction in acromegaly**
Chen Meiping, Zhang Pengyuan, He Wei, et al.
*Pituitary* | May 29, 2026
[PMID: 42213228](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42213228/) | DOI: 10.1007/BF03350250

This cross-sectional study of 74 acromegaly patients used speckle-tracking echocardiography — a sensitive imaging technique that detects subtle heart muscle dysfunction before symptoms appear — to characterize subclinical cardiac abnormalities. IGF-1 z-score was strongly and independently associated with increased left ventricular wall thickness and systolic dyssynchrony, and these effects were significantly amplified in patients with high BMI or elevated triglyceride-glucose index. Male patients showed more pronounced cardiac changes than females. Critically, patients who achieved biochemical remission had measurably better cardiac structure and function than untreated or active disease patients. The findings reinforce that metabolic comorbidities compound the cardiac damage of GH/IGF-1 excess — remission alone may not fully reverse risk — and support early, aggressive cardiometabolic screening as part of standard acromegaly management.

**Osteometabolic complications in patients with secreting pituitary adenomas: Is there an impact of gender?**
di Filippo Luigi, Acanfora Matteo, Bolamperti Simona, et al.
*Pituitary* | May 29, 2026
[PMID: 42213233](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42213233/) | DOI: 10.1007/s11102-024-01397-w

This narrative review synthesizes evidence on skeletal fragility across functioning pituitary adenomas — including acromegaly, Cushing's disease, prolactinomas, and TSH-secreting tumors — with a focus on sex-related differences. A key clinical message is that bone risk is systematically underestimated when clinicians rely solely on standard dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which misses alterations in bone microarchitecture. The authors recommend complementing DEXA with vertebral morphometry and trabecular bone score. Notably, male patients, though less commonly affected, often present later with larger tumors and longer hormonal exposure, resulting in a higher fracture burden than females. The review calls for a sex-aware, proactive approach to fracture risk assessment and treatment in all pituitary adenoma patients — a standard that is rarely achieved in current practice.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**AstraZeneca Posts Subgroup Survival Data for Anselamimab in AL Amyloidosis at ASCO**
*Fierce Biotech* | May 29, 2026
[https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/astrazeneca-after-phase-3-miss-posts-subgroup-data-behind-pitch-rare-disease-approval](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/astrazeneca-after-phase-3-miss-posts-subgroup-data-behind-pitch-rare-disease-approval)

AstraZeneca presented data at ASCO 2026 from a 72-patient prespecified subgroup of its anselamimab Phase 3 trial in light-chain (AL) amyloidosis — a rare disease in which misfolded proteins accumulate in organs. The overall trial missed its primary endpoint, but in the kappa light chain isotype subgroup (roughly 20% of the estimated 74,000 US patients), anselamimab reduced mortality by 62% versus placebo (31.3% vs. 58.3% deaths) and cut cardiovascular hospitalizations by 71%. AstraZeneca is pursuing a regulatory strategy targeting this subgroup — centered on a drug originally developed by Caelum Biosciences — and believes the data are nominally statistically significant and clinically meaningful. The strategy is aggressive but uncertain: subgroup-only approvals face high evidentiary bars, and the competing lambda subgroup showed no benefit whatsoever. This is a critical watch item for rare disease sponsors attempting regulatory salvage after a Phase 3 primary endpoint miss.

*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* Demonstrates both the promise and the peril of subgroup-rescue regulatory strategies — and signals that FDA and EMA appetite for such approaches will be tested in a high-profile disease.

**BioMarin Voxzogo Hits Phase 3 Goal in Hypochondroplasia** *(context piece, May 21, 2026)*
*BioPharma Dive* | May 21, 2026
[https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/biomarin-voxzogo-results-hypochondroplasia-trial-results-fda-application/820873/](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/biomarin-voxzogo-results-hypochondroplasia-trial-results-fda-application/820873/)

BioMarin reported positive Phase 3 results for Voxzogo (vosoritide) in hypochondroplasia, a related but typically milder skeletal dysplasia than achondroplasia. Children on Voxzogo grew faster and showed significant increases in standing height and arm span after one year. BioMarin plans to submit a supplemental FDA application in Q3 2026. The news comes as Voxzogo faces new competition in its original achondroplasia indication following Ascendis Pharma's FDA approval of a once-weekly rival in February. Analysts project peak hypochondroplasia sales of up to $600 million, though the condition is often underdiagnosed and the market will need to be built. The win also came two days after BioMarin's experimental ENPP1 deficiency drug missed a key Phase 3 endpoint — a reminder of the uneven nature of rare disease drug development.

*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* Illustrates how established rare disease players can extend franchise value by expanding approved drugs into related orphan conditions.

**FDA Clinical Hold Halts Aardvark's Prader-Willi Trial on Heart Concerns** *(context piece, May 15, 2026)*
*BioPharma Dive* | May 15, 2026
[https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/aardvark-fda-hold-prader-willi-trial/820338/](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/aardvark-fda-hold-prader-willi-trial/820338/)

The FDA placed a clinical hold on Aardvark Therapeutics' ARD-101 — a gut hormone stimulator being tested for hyperphagia (pathological hunger) in Prader-Willi syndrome — following cardiovascular safety signals observed in a high-dose healthy volunteer study. Aardvark's Phase 3 trial and open-label extension had dosed 87 patients; the company will now unblind the data to help regulators and investigators determine a path forward. Shares fell over 35% and the company faces a funding runway of roughly mid-2027. The situation is distinct from Neurocrine's recently approved Prader-Willi drug Vyktat, which targets a different mechanism. The hold underscores how cardiovascular off-target effects can derail even late-stage rare disease programs when dose-safety relationships are inadequately characterized early.

*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* A cautionary case for dose-selection and cardiosafety characterization in rare disease programs — especially when using novel gut hormone mechanisms.

## What to Watch

In acromegaly, the two Pituitary papers this week reframe disease burden: even in patients with well-controlled GH/IGF-1 levels, the metabolic milieu can silently accelerate cardiovascular and skeletal harm. Clinicians and trialists designing outcomes studies should consider whether remission alone is a sufficient endpoint, or whether metabolic normalization needs to be co-targeted. Meanwhile, the rare disease industry is navigating a complicated regulatory moment: AstraZeneca's ASCO subgroup gambit for AL amyloidosis, BioMarin's franchise extension in hypochondroplasia, and Aardvark's clinical hold all reflect the high-stakes, high-variance nature of rare disease drug development in a period of FDA leadership flux. The common thread — and the one to watch — is whether a changing FDA posture on evidence standards will raise or lower the bar for subgroup-based and surrogate-endpoint-based approvals that rare disease developers depend on.
