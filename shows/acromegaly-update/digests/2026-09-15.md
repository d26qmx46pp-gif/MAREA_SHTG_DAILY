# Acromegaly Update — Tuesday September 15

**Bottom line:** It's a quiet week for acromegaly-specific research — just one review paper worth flagging — but rare disease industry news was busy, headlined by Scholar Rock's first-ever muscle-targeted SMA approval and a rough week for two RNA-based rare disease programs at Novartis.

## Part A — Acromegaly research

Only one paper met the top-tier journal bar this week; it's a review rather than new trial data, but it's a genuinely useful synthesis of an under-discussed complication.

**Acromegalic Osteoarthropathy: A New Paradigm of Pathological Musculoskeletal Remodeling.** Mazziotti G, Minisola S, Canalis E. *European Journal of Endocrinology*, published online Sept 15, 2026. [PMID 42736608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42736608) | [DOI: 10.1093/ejendo/lvag178](https://doi.org/10.1093/ejendo/lvag178)

This review reframes the bone and joint disease seen in acromegaly patients as a single, interconnected process rather than two separate problems (bone fragility and arthropathy). The authors argue that bone, joints, muscle, and body composition all remodel together in response to years of excess growth hormone, and that this musculoskeletal damage often persists even after a patient's endocrine disease is successfully treated. They walk through current tools for assessing bone quality, vertebral fractures, and joint damage, and note real limits — conventional bone density scans don't capture the full fracture risk in these patients. The practical takeaway is a call to shift from "treat the hormone and move on" to lifelong, phenotype-driven surveillance of the whole musculoskeletal system. It's a conceptual piece rather than new clinical data, but it's a useful roadmap for endocrinologists managing long-term acromegaly patients.

No other acromegaly papers cleared the top-tier journal bar this week — a genuinely thin week on the research side.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**Scholar Rock wins FDA approval for first muscle-targeted SMA drug.** STAT News, Sept 11, 2026. [statnews.com](https://www.statnews.com/2026/09/11/scholar-rock-spinal-muscular-atrophy-isembyld-clinical-trial-fda-approval/)

The FDA approved Scholar Rock's Isembyld (apitegromab) for use alongside existing SMN2-targeting therapies in adults and children 2 and older with spinal muscular atrophy — the first-ever therapy designed specifically to target muscle loss in SMA rather than the underlying genetic defect. In the pivotal trial, patients on Isembyld plus a standard SMA therapy saw motor skills improve over a year, while a placebo group declined, a statistically significant difference. The approval does carry a fracture-risk warning that wasn't previously flagged in the drug's trial data. *Why it matters for rare disease developers:* it validates myostatin inhibition — a mechanism with a long history of industry-wide failures — as a viable add-on strategy for neuromuscular disease, and sets a template for "muscle-targeted" therapies layered on top of gene-targeted treatments.

**Novartis' $12B RNA drug bet misses in rare muscle disease trial.** BioPharma Dive, Sept 8, 2026. [biopharmadive.com](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/novartis-rna-drug-failure-dm1-avidity-del-desiran/829778/)

Del-desiran, the lead RNA therapy Novartis acquired through its $12 billion buyout of Avidity Biosciences, failed to beat placebo on its main goal in a Phase 3 trial for myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1), a rare muscle-wasting genetic disease. Novartis says it saw "evidence of clinical activity" on secondary and exploratory measures and plans to discuss next steps with regulators, but analysts read the miss as a real setback — one had priced in an 80% success probability and $1.5B in peak sales. The read-through hit a competitor too: Dyne Therapeutics, developing a similar DM1 therapy that uses the same muscle-relaxation test, saw its own success odds marked down by at least one analyst. *Why it matters for rare disease developers:* it's a reminder that big-ticket rare disease acquisitions carry real clinical risk, and that trial endpoint choice (here, a variable hand-relaxation test) can sink an otherwise well-funded program.

**BrainChild Bio raises $116M for CAR-T therapies targeting childhood brain cancer.** BioPharma Dive, Sept 8, 2026. [biopharmadive.com](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/brainchild-pediatric-brain-tumors-car-t/829463/)

BrainChild Bio, spun out of Seattle Children's Hospital, closed a $116 million Series A led by an undisclosed private family fund and foundation, with Seattle Children's and the Washington Research Foundation's venture arm also participating. The money funds a Phase 2 trial of BCB-276, a CAR-T therapy for diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG) — a brainstem tumor that strikes roughly 300 U.S. children a year and kills most within two years of diagnosis. The therapy targets B7-H3 and, notably, doesn't require chemotherapy conditioning beforehand, which the company says helps keep the engineered cells in the brain and lowers systemic side-effect risk. A second program for glioblastoma is slated to enter human testing next year. *Why it matters for rare disease developers:* it's a sizable vote of confidence in an unfashionable niche — pediatric-only rare cancers with limited commercial upside — and in cell therapy's potential to cross the blood-brain barrier.

## What to watch

The Novartis and Scholar Rock news this week are a study in contrasts: one company's approach to neuromuscular disease just cleared the FDA, another's just face-planted in a related muscle disorder, and the failure specifically calls into question a clinical endpoint (hand-relaxation testing) that at least one other DM1 competitor is relying on — worth watching whether that ripples further. On the acromegaly side, keep an eye out for the review's call to formalize long-term musculoskeletal surveillance protocols; if that gains traction, it could shape how acromegaly patients are followed even after biochemical control is achieved. Also on the radar: Ultragenyx's Sanfilippo syndrome gene therapy has a PDUFA date of September 19 — just days out — which would be a good candidate for next week's rare disease roundup regardless of outcome.
