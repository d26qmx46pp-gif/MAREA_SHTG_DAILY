**Cost-effectiveness of pasireotide long-acting release in acromegaly: a systematic literature review and methodology assessment.** Gilis-Januszewska A, Bronikowska M, Binowski G. *Journal of Medical Economics.* [PMID: 41518621](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41518621/) | [DOI: 10.1080/13696998.2025.2609506](https://doi.org/10.1080/13696998.2025.2609506)

Pasireotide long-acting release (LAR) is a second-line option for acromegaly patients who don't respond well to surgery or first-generation somatostatin receptor ligands, but how it stacks up financially against the other major second-line option, pegvisomant, has been murky. This review combed four databases and found only six studies worldwide that have actually tried to answer the cost-effectiveness question, and their conclusions varied widely. Rather than average those studies together, the authors instead picked apart *why* they disagree — differences in modeling assumptions, data sources, and how well each model matched real clinical practice. The upshot: the existing cost-effectiveness evidence for pasireotide LAR is thin and methodologically inconsistent, which matters for payers and health systems trying to decide which second-line drug to favor.

**A rare case of an indolent giant GH-secreting pituitary adenoma.** Shamim S, Furqan S, Ahmad MN. *JCEM Case Reports.* [PMID: 42529153](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42529153/) | [DOI: 10.1210/jcemcr/luag138](https://doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag138)

A case report on a giant growth-hormone-secreting pituitary adenoma — the tumor type that causes acromegaly — that behaved unusually indolently despite its large size. Full abstract text wasn't yet available at time of writing (the record is freshly indexed), but giant GH-secreting adenomas are notable because their size doesn't always predict disease severity or treatment difficulty, and case reports like this one help refine how endocrinologists think about the size-versus-behavior relationship in acromegaly.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**AstraZeneca's ATTR-CM heart drug fails Phase 3, sending ripples through Alnylam and BridgeBio.** STAT News, Aug. 28, 2026. [statnews.com/2026/08/28/astrazeneca-ionis-wainua-eplontersen-attr-cm-study-failure-bridgebio-alnylam](https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/28/astrazeneca-ionis-wainua-eplontersen-attr-cm-study-failure-bridgebio-alnylam/)

AstraZeneca and Ionis Pharmaceuticals detailed why their "silencer" drug for transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM), a rare and progressive heart disease, surprise-failed its Phase 3 trial: patients already taking a different class of drug called a stabilizer saw no added benefit from the silencer, and since most trial participants were already on a stabilizer, the effect tanked the whole study. The result has knock-on implications for the broader ATTR-CM competitive field — Alnylam sells a rival silencer (Amvuttra), and BridgeBio makes a stabilizer — with some analysts reading the data as evidence that oral stabilizers may have the edge over injectable silencers in this disease. *Why it matters for rare disease developers:* ATTR-CM is a crowded, high-value rare disease market, and a single trial's mechanistic finding just reshuffled how investors and clinicians weigh two entire drug classes against each other.

**Bayer, Takeda and CSL Behring join new White House drug-pricing deals.** STAT News (Pharmalot), Aug. 31, 2026. [statnews.com/pharmalot/2026/08/31/drug-pricing-deals-polycythemia-vera-mimrylo-pharmalittle-newsletter](https://www.statnews.com/pharmalot/2026/08/31/drug-pricing-deals-polycythemia-vera-mimrylo-pharmalittle-newsletter/)

Bayer, Takeda, CSL Behring and several mid-sized drugmakers are reportedly part of a new round of drug-pricing agreements the White House was expected to announce, per Financial Times reporting cited by STAT. These deals continue a pattern from the Trump administration of trading tariff relief for drug-pricing concessions from manufacturers, though how substantive the concessions actually are has remained unclear in prior rounds. *Why it matters for rare disease developers:* CSL Behring is one of the largest rare-disease and gene-therapy manufacturers in the world (hemophilia, primary immunodeficiency), so pricing terms it agrees to here could set a template other rare-disease drugmakers are pressured to follow.
