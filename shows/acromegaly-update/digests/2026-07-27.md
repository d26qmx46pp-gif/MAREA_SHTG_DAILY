# Acromegaly Update — Monday July 27

**Bottom line:** A trio of new Pituitary papers built out the real-world case for oral paltusotine in controlled acromegaly, while on the industry side a small rare-disease repurposing startup inked a licensing deal and orphan drugmakers pushed back on Medicare price-pilot exposure.

## Part A — Acromegaly research

**Reduced breakthrough symptom exacerbations in patients with biochemically controlled acromegaly switched from injected depot somatostatin receptor ligands to once-daily oral paltusotine in the PATHFNDR-1 clinical trial**
Clemmons DR, Quock TP, Casagrande A, Wang Y, Krasner A. *Pituitary* (2026). [PMID: 42493688](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42493688/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01733-2)
This is a secondary analysis of the phase 3 PATHFNDR-1 trial, which tested switching acromegaly patients who were already biochemically controlled on injected depot somatostatin receptor ligands (SRLs) over to once-daily oral paltusotine. IGF-1 levels and average symptom severity scores stayed essentially flat after the switch, so on paper nothing changed. But when the researchers looked at "breakthrough" symptom spikes — days where a symptom jumped by 2 or more points on a 10-point scale — those exacerbations dropped from roughly 30% of days on injected SRLs to about 6% of days on paltusotine. In other words, patients experienced far fewer bad days even though their average biochemical control looked the same, suggesting IGF-1 alone under-represents how patients actually feel day to day. That's a meaningful data point for an oral drug trying to displace injectable SRLs as the maintenance standard.

**Symptom severity and exacerbation frequency in medically treated patients with acromegaly**
Geer EB, Clemmons DR, Sisco J, et al. *Pituitary* (2026). [PMID: 42493640](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42493640/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01732-3)
A companion analysis from the same research group, this one is a baseline natural-history study of 31 patients on injected depot SRLs, tracked with daily symptom diaries for three months. It found that symptom exacerbations happened on roughly a third of all days during the injection cycle, and that three-month patient recall tended to overestimate symptom severity while completely missing how often those flare days occurred. Worse exacerbation frequency tracked with worse daily functioning, lower treatment satisfaction, and more healthcare utilization. Read together with the PATHFNDR-1 paper above, this pair makes the case that the injection cycle itself — not just average IGF-1 control — is a meaningful source of symptom burden that a once-daily oral option could address.

**Ex vivo drug sensitivity in patient-derived 3D cultures in acromegaly and its association with clinical predictors**
Tsujimoto Y, Ishida A, Costa da Silva FG, et al. *Pituitary* (2026). [PMID: 42496773](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42496773/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01735-0)
A Japanese group grew spheroid-based 3D cultures from tumor tissue taken from 27 acromegaly patients and exposed them to octreotide, cabergoline, and pasireotide to see whether lab-dish drug response could predict which patients would actually respond clinically. All three drugs modestly reduced tumor cell viability in roughly a third to 40% of samples, and — encouragingly — the lab responders lined up with markers doctors already use, like MRI signal characteristics and pathology patterns. This is early, exploratory, mechanistic work rather than anything clinic-ready, but it's a step toward a personalized-medicine tool that could one day help pick the right medical therapy for a given patient before months are spent on trial and error.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**Rare disease startup inks $31M deal for Chinese drug, eyes $50M fundraise**
Fierce Biotech, July 22, 2026. [Source](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/rare-disease-startup-inks-31m-deal-chinese-drug-thiel-backed-ceo-eyes-50m-fundraise)
Ashlins Pharmaceuticals, a five-person startup founded by CEO Jennifer Lin (a Thiel Fellowship recipient), licensed the ex-China rights to a Hong Kong-based Lee's Pharmaceutical formulation of interferon alpha-2b — the active ingredient once sold as Merck's Intron A before Merck discontinued it commercially in 2022, leaving rare-disease patients who used it off-label without a domestic source. The deal is worth up to $31 million in upfront and milestone payments, terms undisclosed beyond that ceiling. Ashlins plans to pursue formal FDA approval for a specific rare-disease indication it hasn't yet named, has a pre-IND meeting with the FDA scheduled for early August, and is now targeting an October raise of roughly $50 million to fund the program through approval.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* It's a small deal, but it's a clean example of the "repurpose an orphaned but clinically validated drug" playbook that a growing crop of thinly capitalized startups are using to get de-risked assets to rare-disease patients without originating new chemistry.

**Rare disease drugmakers seek exclusion from Medicare pricing pilots**
STAT News, July 21, 2026. [Source](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/21/rare-disease-coalition-orphan-drug-exemption-medicare-pilot-price-programs/)
The Rare Disease Company Coalition met with the White House Office of Management and Budget last week to lobby for orphan drugs to be excluded from two new federal pilot programs tied to the Trump administration's "most-favored nation" push to bring U.S. drug prices in line with other wealthy countries. The coalition's argument is that rare-disease therapeutics, which often serve tiny patient populations and carry correspondingly high per-patient development and manufacturing costs, don't fit the same pricing logic as mass-market drugs and could see specific development disincentivized if swept into the same pilots.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* Any carve-out (or its absence) from these pilots could materially change the economics of launching an orphan drug in the U.S. over the next few years — worth watching closely as OMB and CMS finalize pilot scope.

**Agios discontinues sickle cell drug tebapivat after Phase 2 miss**
STAT News, July 21, 2026 (The Readout). [Source](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/21/biotech-news-anthropic-deepens-work-with-rare-disease-drugs/)
Agios Pharmaceuticals is discontinuing development of tebapivat, a next-generation candidate for sickle cell disease, after a Phase 2 study failed to establish a "differentiated profile to support continued development," per the company.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* A reminder that even validated mechanisms (Agios's pyruvate kinase activation platform already has an approved sickle cell drug, Pyrukynd) don't guarantee next-gen candidates clear the bar — pipeline discontinuations like this reshape competitive maps in hemoglobinopathy treatment.

## What to watch

The acromegaly data this week reinforces a theme playing out across rare endocrine and kidney disease alike: regulators and payers increasingly care about more than average biomarker control, and companies that can show reduced day-to-day symptom burden (paltusotine) or faster, cleaner regulatory paths (the ongoing Medicare-pilot carve-out fight) are positioning themselves ahead of the pack. Worth watching whether the Rare Disease Company Coalition's Medicare exemption push gains traction, since a favorable outcome would ease pricing pressure on maintenance therapies across acromegaly, IgAN, and other orphan-disease categories simultaneously.
