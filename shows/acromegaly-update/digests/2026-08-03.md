# Acromegaly Update — Monday August 3

**Bottom line:** It's a quiet week for acromegaly-specific research — no trials, just incremental papers on AI-assisted detection, surgical outcomes, and pediatric gigantism — while the broader rare disease industry saw a gene-editing "gold rush" for a liver/lung disorder, a financing bet against two bigger rivals in Angelman syndrome, and a pulmonary hypertension partnership fall apart after a near-miss trial.

## Part A — Acromegaly research

**Role of Artificial Intelligence in acromegaly detection and management**
Kasuki L, Ferreira da Gama EM, Wildemberg LE. *The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism*, 2026. [PMID: 42535540](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42535540/) | [DOI: 10.1210/clinem/dgag311](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgag311)
This review lays out where AI could actually move the needle in acromegaly, a disease notorious for diagnostic delays that often stretch past 6 to 10 years. The authors highlight AI-assisted analysis of facial and hand photos as a way to catch the disease's subtle physical changes earlier, before a patient ends up bouncing between specialists. They also survey machine learning models that predict surgical remission, drug response, and radiotherapy outcomes — often outperforming single biomarkers on their own. It's a review, not new data, but it's a useful snapshot of where the field's diagnostic and treatment-selection tools are headed.

**Perioperative predictors of biochemical remission in acromegaly: a predictive modeling study**
Sun H, Patel B, Lohana S, Sarkar R, Chiapponi M. *Pituitary*, 2026. [PMID: 42509491](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42509491/) | [DOI: 10.1007/s11102-026-01725-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01725-2)
Looking back at 86 patients who had endoscopic surgery for GH-secreting pituitary tumors, this study found that about 72% reached biochemical remission. The factors that predicted success were older patient age, a rounder tumor shape, complete tumor removal, a lower Knosp grade (a measure of how much the tumor has invaded surrounding structures), and a specific staining pattern on pathology. Their best machine learning model combined four of those features and correctly sorted remission versus non-remission about 85% of the time in testing. It's a modest, single-institution analysis, but it points toward better tools for counseling patients before surgery about their odds.

**Higher surgical cure of pediatric gigantism with endoscopic endonasal surgery: case series and review of the literature**
Garton ALA, Guadix SW, Vuguin PM, Greenfield JP, Schwartz TH. *Pituitary*, 2026. [PMID: 42538445](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42538445/) | [DOI: 10.1007/s11102-026-01738-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01738-x)
Pediatric gigantism — acromegaly's rare childhood counterpart, caused by the same GH-secreting tumors before growth plates close — is vanishingly uncommon, and this paper is a reminder of just how thin the evidence base is. The authors describe three children treated with modern endoscopic surgery, all of whom reached remission without complications, then pooled those cases with 19 prior studies covering 325 children total. Across that pooled literature, overall surgical cure rates were only about 25%, though newer endoscopic techniques appear to outperform older microsurgical approaches. It's a small case series dressed up with a literature review, but pediatric gigantism is so rare that even this counts as meaningful evidence.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**From 'lost cause' to gold rush: biotechs swarm to cure a rare liver and lung disease**
STAT News, July 28, 2026. [statnews.com/2026/07/28/biotech-industry-race-aatd-cure-gene-editing-alpha-1-antitrypsin-deficiency](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/28/biotech-industry-race-aatd-cure-gene-editing-alpha-1-antitrypsin-deficiency/)
Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, a rare genetic disorder that damages the liver and lungs, has become the hottest target in gene editing almost by accident: it's caused by a single DNA misspelling, affects a commercially attractive population of roughly 200,000 people, and is treatable through the liver — the one organ current gene-editing tools can reliably reach. Beam Therapeutics leads with base-editing data showing sharp increases in properly folded protein at high doses, but it's now fighting on multiple fronts: an arbitration loss to Prime Medicine, competition from Regeneron and Tessera, and a fast-moving Shanghai biotech, Yoltech, that just landed a licensing deal with RA Capital and RTW that triggered accusations of enabling a "copycat." Wave Life Sciences and Korro Bio are pursuing a non-DNA-editing RNA approach, though their early data has lagged Beam's.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* it's a case study in how a single well-chosen rare disease can become a multi-company gold rush — and a preview of the patent fights, geopolitics, and pricing questions that other gene-editing programs will eventually face.

**After missed endpoint, Gossamer and Chiesi pull the plug on PAH drug collaboration**
Fierce Biotech, July 27, 2026. [fiercebiotech.com/biotech/after-missed-endpoint-gossamer-and-chiesi-pull-plug-pah-drug-collaboration](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/after-missed-endpoint-gossamer-and-chiesi-pull-plug-pah-drug-collaboration)
Gossamer Bio and Italian drugmaker Chiesi have unwound their roughly $486 million partnership on seralutinib, a candidate for pulmonary arterial hypertension, a rare and often fatal narrowing of the lung's blood vessels. The termination comes after a phase 3 trial in February narrowly missed its prespecified statistical bar (a p-value of 0.032 against a 0.025 threshold) on six-minute walk distance, even though secondary endpoints favored the drug in sicker patients. Gossamer regains full worldwide rights and, encouraged by FDA meeting minutes that characterized the miss as a "review issue" rather than an outright barrier, plans to file for approval in September anyway.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* it's a live test of how much latitude the FDA will give a rare-disease drug that technically missed its primary endpoint — a question with implications well beyond PAH.

**RA Capital backs upstart rival to Ultragenyx and Ionis in rare brain disease**
STAT News, July 27, 2026. [statnews.com/2026/07/27/ra-captial-oak-hill-bio-spac-ultragenyx-ionis-angelman-syndrome](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/27/ra-captial-oak-hill-bio-spac-ultragenyx-ionis-angelman-syndrome/)
RA Capital and other investors are putting $175 million behind Oak Hill Bio, a new company reviving an Angelman syndrome drug that Roche had shelved. Angelman syndrome is a rare genetic neurodevelopmental disorder, and Oak Hill is stepping into a field where Ultragenyx and Ionis are already running their own phase 3 trials of competing drugs — with Ultragenyx expected to post results later this year. (Coverage is paywalled beyond this point, so mechanism-of-action and trial-design specifics aren't confirmed here.)
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* it shows investors are still willing to fund a third or fourth entrant into a rare-disease category once clinical proof-of-concept looks close, rather than waiting for a single winner to emerge.

## What to watch

The throughline this week is competitive crowding: acromegaly's own diagnostic and surgical literature is inching toward AI-assisted precision rather than new drugs, while three separate corners of rare disease — gene editing for AATD, pulmonary hypertension, and Angelman syndrome — are all seeing multiple well-funded companies chase the same target simultaneously. Worth watching next: whether the FDA's apparent leniency on Gossamer's missed PAH endpoint becomes a template other rare-disease sponsors try to invoke, and whether Ultragenyx's expected Angelman syndrome readout this year reshapes the field before Oak Hill's revived Roche asset even reaches the clinic.
