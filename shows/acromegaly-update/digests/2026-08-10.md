# Acromegaly Update — Monday August 10

**Bottom line:** It's a quiet week for acromegaly-specific trial data — the research side leans mechanistic and clinical-management, headlined by a novel long-acting GH receptor antagonist — while rare disease industry news brought a fresh Phase 2 win for Silence Therapeutics, an $800M Stargardt disease acquisition, a CRISPR safety insight from Intellia, and a Washington fight over whether orphan drugs get carved out of Medicare price pilots.

*Note: no top-tier journal (NEJM/Lancet/JAMA-tier) hits met the bar this week for Part A — the query was broadened to include mid-tier endocrinology and case-report journals, flagged below.*

## Part A — Acromegaly research

**Development and Characterization of a Long-Acting Allosteric Growth Hormone Receptor Antagonist for Acromegaly**
Kurylo K, Bouchard PR, Milton MN. *Endocrinology*, Aug 2026. [PMID 42563406](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42563406/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1210/endocr/bqag090)
Pegvisomant is currently the only approved growth hormone receptor antagonist for acromegaly, but it requires daily injections that many patients struggle to keep up with in practice. This preclinical paper describes MAR002, a new half-life-extended monoclonal antibody designed to block the same receptor with much less frequent dosing. Researchers made it by immunizing mice with the human and monkey growth hormone receptor and then characterizing the resulting antibody's binding and blocking activity. It's early-stage work — no human data yet — but it's a notable entrant in the small field of GH receptor antagonists aimed at making second-line acromegaly treatment more tolerable.

**Somatic Genetic Alterations in Pituitary Neuroendocrine Tumors**
Schwarz Y, Halperin R, Tirosh A. *Endocrine-Related Cancer*, Aug 2026. [PMID 42565462](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42565462/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1530/ERC-26-0213)
This review pulls together what's known about the genetic mutations that drive pituitary tumors, including the GH-secreting (somatotroph) tumors that cause acromegaly. The authors highlight GNAS as the most frequent driver mutation in somatotroph tumors, alongside USP8, USP48, and BRAF in the corticotroph tumors that cause Cushing's disease. It's a useful state-of-the-field summary rather than new data, but it underscores how much of acromegaly's underlying biology is now understood at the molecular level — which matters for future targeted therapies.

**Macrophage-secreted factors promote invasiveness in somatotroph PitNETs: Cathepsin Z as a macrophage-associated marker**
Chen M, Duan L, Miao H, et al. *Molecular and Cellular Endocrinology*, Nov 2026 (online ahead of print). [PMID 42476336](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42476336/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mce.2026.112874)
Invasive GH-secreting pituitary tumors tend to recur and resist treatment more than non-invasive ones, but why some tumors turn invasive isn't well understood. This lab study finds that immune cells called macrophages, which infiltrate these tumors, secrete a protein called Cathepsin Z that appears to help tumor cells invade surrounding tissue. The correlation between Cathepsin Z levels and worse outcomes was previously seen in the same research group's proteomics work; this paper digs into the mechanism. It's basic science, not something that changes practice today, but it points to a possible new biomarker or drug target for the more aggressive, treatment-resistant acromegaly cases.

**Acromegaly and Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease: Clinical and Therapeutic Implications**
Polyzos SA, Mantovani A, Targher G. *Current Obesity Reports*, Aug 2026. [PMID 42554920](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42554920/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42000-021-00339-6)
This narrative review looks at the relationship between acromegaly and fatty liver disease (now called MASLD). Counterintuitively, growth hormone excess can actually protect against liver fat buildup in some patients by acting on liver cells and the molecules that drive scarring, but the picture is complicated once insulin resistance and other metabolic effects of acromegaly are factored in. The authors argue this has real treatment implications: clinicians managing acromegaly should be thinking about liver health as part of the broader metabolic picture, not treating it as a separate issue.

**Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy With Defibrillator for Acromegalic Cardiomyopathy With Left Bundle Branch Block**
Bai J, Shang Z, Cui M, et al. *JACC: Case Reports*, Aug 2026. [PMID 42213007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42213007/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccas.2026.108591)
A case report on a 56-year-old man whose acromegaly-related heart failure didn't fully resolve even after his hormone levels were brought under control with somatostatin analog therapy. He went on to develop acute heart failure with a bundle branch block — an electrical conduction problem — and was successfully treated with a cardiac resynchronization device with defibrillator backup. It's a single case, but it's a useful reminder for endocrinologists and cardiologists that acromegalic cardiomyopathy sometimes needs device-based heart failure treatment on top of hormone control, not instead of it.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**Silence Therapeutics aims at Takeda with new Phase 2 data in a rare blood cancer**
STAT News, Aug 10, 2026. [Source](https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/10/silence-polycythemia-vera-trial-results-divesiran-takeda-rusfertide/)
Silence Therapeutics reported that its RNA interference drug divesiran stabilized red blood cell levels in a 48-person Phase 2 trial for polycythemia vera, a rare blood cancer marked by dangerous overproduction of red blood cells. Eighty-eight percent of patients dosed every six or twelve weeks achieved a clinical response, versus 19% on placebo. The result positions divesiran as a potential competitor to rusfertide, a Takeda drug that's further along toward approval for the same disease.
Why it matters for rare disease developers: it's a reminder that being second to a novel mechanism in a rare disease doesn't mean losing the race — strong Phase 2 data on dosing convenience (6- or 12-week intervals) can still carve out a competitive position against a more advanced rival.

**Alkeus, an unorthodox eye drug startup, acquired by Tarsus for up to $800M**
BioPharma Dive, Aug 6, 2026. [Source](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/alkeus-tarsus-acquire-stargardt-drug-startup-boger/827243/)
Tarsus Pharmaceuticals agreed to acquire Alkeus Pharmaceuticals, a startup founded by former Vertex executive Josh Boger, in a deal worth up to $800 million. Alkeus has been developing a late-stage therapy for Stargardt disease, an inherited, currently untreatable form of vision loss that typically begins in childhood or adolescence.
Why it matters for rare disease developers: it's another data point in a busy year for rare-disease M&A, and a sign that acquirers are still willing to pay up for late-stage assets in ultra-rare conditions with no approved treatment, even from small, founder-led biotechs.

**Intellia says it may have found why some patients get liver damage from its CRISPR drug**
BioPharma Dive, Aug 7, 2026. [Source](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/daraxonrasib-compassionate-use-intellia-arrowhead-cspc-vedanta/827186/)
Intellia Therapeutics said an analysis of nearly 600 patient samples found that carriers of a specific genetic marker, the HLA-C*05:01 allele, had the largest liver enzyme elevations after dosing with its CRISPR gene-editing therapy nex-z for transthyretin amyloidosis, the disease behind last year's clinical hold after a patient's liver injury and death. The finding doesn't yet change the trials' regulatory status, but it gives Intellia a possible way to flag higher-risk patients going forward.
Why it matters for rare disease developers: liver toxicity has been one of the biggest safety overhangs on in-vivo CRISPR therapies; a genetic marker that predicts risk — if it holds up — could let the field manage that risk with screening rather than abandoning promising programs.

**Exempting rare disease drugs from Medicare pricing pilots would erase most of the savings, analysis finds**
STAT News, Aug 5, 2026. [Source](https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/05/cms-drug-pricing-pilot-programs-rare-disease-drugs/)
Biotech companies are lobbying the Trump administration to keep orphan drugs out of two Medicare pilot programs — GLOBE and GUARD — designed to bring U.S. drug prices closer to those in other wealthy countries. A Harvard researcher's new analysis found that carving out rare disease therapies would wipe out much of the pilots' projected savings, since orphan drugs make up an outsized share of Medicare spending relative to their patient volumes.
Why it matters for rare disease developers: the outcome of this lobbying fight will shape how exposed — or protected — orphan drug pricing is under the administration's broader most-favored-nation pricing push, with direct revenue implications for every rare disease company with a Medicare-covered product.

## What to watch

Nothing this week directly connects the two segments, but there's a shared thread: both acromegaly research and the broader rare disease industry are wrestling with the tension between mechanism-level progress — a new GH receptor antagonist, a CRISPR safety biomarker — and the slower, harder work of turning that progress into affordable, durable patient access. Keep an eye on the GLOBE/GUARD pricing pilot fight in Washington; however it resolves will set a precedent that touches every orphan drug, acromegaly therapies included.
