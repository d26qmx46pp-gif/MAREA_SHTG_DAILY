# Acromegaly Update — Monday August 24

**Bottom line:** It was a quiet week for acromegaly-specific research — three observational studies refined how we monitor and support patients rather than changing treatment — while the wider rare disease industry saw two major FDA approvals and two sizable M&A deals land within days of each other.

## Part A — Acromegaly research

**Soluble alpha-klotho as an adjunct biomarker of disease activity in acromegaly: Added value beyond GH and IGF-1**
Jha V, Medenica S, Sachdeva N. *Pituitary.* [PMID: 42622967](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42622967/) | DOI: [10.1007/s11102-017-0838-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11102-017-0838-2)
This case-control study of 80 acromegaly patients and 80 controls found that blood levels of soluble alpha-klotho track disease activity even when growth hormone and IGF-1 give conflicting readings. Klotho levels rose in step with disease severity — lowest in well-controlled patients, highest in newly diagnosed, active disease — and a klotho cutoff of about 1,580 pg/mL identified uncontrolled disease with strong accuracy. Adding klotho to standard markers modestly improved diagnostic performance. The authors are careful to frame it as a supplementary tool for ambiguous cases, not a replacement for GH, IGF-1, and clinical judgment.

**Beyond biochemical control: factors associated with quality of life in patients with acromegaly**
Kurt AMO, Çaklılı ÖT, Bektaş F. *Pituitary.* [PMID: 42622888](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42622888/) | DOI: [10.3389/fendo.2021.627711](https://doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2021.627711)
In a cross-sectional study of 90 patients — 86 of whom were already in biochemical remission — quality of life still varied widely, and the strongest predictor wasn't a lab value at all: it was depressive symptom burden. Women reported lower quality of life and higher anxiety and depression than men, but that sex gap disappeared once depression scores were accounted for. Higher education level was independently linked to better quality of life. The takeaway is that hitting biochemical targets doesn't guarantee patients feel well, and routine screening for mood symptoms may matter as much as chasing IGF-1 numbers.

**The link between acromegaly, liver morphology, and MASLD: a large cohort analysis**
Ercan P, Yazgan BF, Sendur SN. *European Journal of Endocrinology.* [PMID: 42615838](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42615838/) | DOI: [10.1093/ejendo/lvag159](https://doi.org/10.1093/ejendo/lvag159)
A four-decade single-center cohort of 394 acromegaly patients found a surprising split in liver findings: hepatic cysts showed up at rates matching the general population, but fatty liver disease (MASLD) was actually less common than expected in imaged patients. Higher GH/IGF-1 exposure — both at the time of imaging and averaged over time — was associated with lower MASLD risk, while cysts tracked with older age and kidney cysts instead. The authors describe the inverse relationship between cysts and fatty liver as "hypothesis-generating," suggesting two distinct hepatic patterns rather than a single hormone-driven process.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**FDA approves Ultragenyx's first gene therapy, for a rare glycogen storage disease**
BioPharma Dive, Aug. 20, 2026. [biopharmadive.com](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/ultragenyx-fda-approve-genglycos-GSDIa-gene-therapy/828369/)
The FDA cleared Ultragenyx's Glenglycos, a one-time gene therapy for glycogen storage disease type Ia, a condition that leaves patients without an enzyme needed to stabilize blood sugar between meals. It's Ultragenyx's fifth approved product and first marketable gene therapy, priced at $2.7 million per patient, and comes bundled with a priority review voucher the company plans to sell — recent vouchers have fetched $180–200 million. The approval was granted on an accelerated basis, so longer-term data on clinical benefit is still to come.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* It's a concrete example of the voucher system working as an economic bridge for a company that's had a rocky few years, and a reminder that "rare" doesn't always mean "blockbuster" — analysts expect peak sales of only around $362 million.

**Regeneron's FOP drug Pasatru wins FDA approval after a three-decade effort**
STAT News, Aug. 19, 2026. [statnews.com](https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/19/regeneron-fop-garetosmab-fda-approval-pasatru/)
Regeneron's garetosmab, now branded Pasatru, was approved for fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP), the ultra-rare disease in which the body forms bone in muscle and connective tissue. In the pivotal trial, the drug sharply cut the rate of new bone formation. Patients with FOP typically lose mobility and become wheelchair-dependent by their mid-20s, and researchers hope the drug can change that trajectory.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* It's a rare disease approval built on a genuinely novel mechanism after decades of failed attempts, underscoring that some of these programs require multi-decade patience before they pay off.

**Leo Pharma buys rare skin disease drug in $435M deal**
BioPharma Dive, Aug. 18, 2026. [biopharmadive.com](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/leo-pharma-buys-late-stage-skin-disease-drug-435m-deal/828150/)
Leo Pharma is acquiring rights to dersimelagon from Bain-owned Tanabe Pharma, an oral drug already under FDA review as the first potential treatment for two rare photosensitivity disorders, erythropoietic protoporphyria and X-linked protoporphyria. The deal includes up to $435 million in upfront and near-term milestones plus royalties. A Phase 3 study showed the drug let patients tolerate more sunlight with fewer painful episodes.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* It shows a private dermatology company using M&A to build out a late-stage rare disease pipeline ahead of a possible IPO — a financing path other privately held rare disease developers may watch closely.

**BioMarin acquires Alesta Therapeutics and its rare bone disorder drug for $275M**
BioPharma Dive, Aug. 18, 2026. [biopharmadive.com](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/biomarin-alesta-deal-acquisition-rare-disease-muscle-skeleton/828133/)
BioMarin is paying $275 million upfront (up to $215 million more in milestones) for Alesta Therapeutics and its lead candidate ALE1, a Phase 1/2 oral therapy for hypophosphatasia, a genetic bone-mineralization disorder. It's BioMarin's third rare disease acquisition in two years, following the $4.8 billion Amicus Therapeutics buyout and the $270 million Inozyme Pharma deal.
*Why it matters for rare disease developers:* BioMarin is explicitly signaling it wants more early-stage rare disease bolt-ons, which is a useful data point for smaller biotechs and their investors weighing a build-vs-sell strategy.

## What to watch

None of this week's acromegaly research changes clinical practice on its own, but the quality-of-life paper is a useful nudge: as more rare disease drugs (including two approved this week) reach patients, the industry's attention is increasingly shifting from "can we treat the biochemistry" to "does the patient actually feel better" — a theme that shows up in both the acromegaly QoL data and in the muted, hedged tone of this week's accelerated approvals. Also worth watching: Ultragenyx has two more high-stakes catalysts due this fall (a Sanfilippo syndrome gene therapy decision in September, and Angelman syndrome trial data expected September–October), which will say a lot about whether this week's approval marks a turnaround or a one-off.
