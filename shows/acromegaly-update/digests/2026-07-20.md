# Acromegaly Update — Monday July 20

**Bottom line:** A quiet week for acromegaly research was anchored by a strong AI-diagnosis paper in JCEM, while rare disease headlines swung between a startup's ultrasound gene-therapy bombshell and a somber earnings-style readout on a rare endocrine disorder.

## Part A — Acromegaly research

**Automatic acromegaly detection using deep learning on hand images: a multicenter observational study**
Ohmachi Y, Nishio M, Abe I, et al. *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* (July 15, 2026)
[PMID: 41757900](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41757900/) | [DOI: 10.1210/clinem/dgag027](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgag027)

A 15-center Japanese team trained a deep learning model (ResNet-50) to spot acromegaly from photos of patients' hands — specifically the dorsal side and the "fist sign," a swelling pattern that's a known but under-recognized clue. Using more than 11,000 images from 716 patients, the model hit 89% sensitivity and 91% specificity, outperforming endocrinologists reviewing the same images (whose F1-scores ranged from a weak 0.43 to 0.63 versus the model's 0.89). Because it works on plain photos of hands rather than faces, the approach sidesteps the privacy concerns that have dogged facial-recognition screening tools. The authors frame it as a potential public health-checkup screening tool, though they caution it needs validation in more diverse populations before real-world deployment. Diagnostic delay is one of acromegaly's biggest problems — this is a concrete, low-friction step toward catching it earlier.

**LINAC-based Stereotactic Radiotherapy for Acromegaly: A Single-center Retrospective Cohort**
Mauro GP, Da Róz LM, Gico VC, et al. *Journal of Neurological Surgery Part B: Skull Base* (2026)
[PMID: 42404180](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42404180/) | [DOI: 10.1055/a-2693-1815](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2693-1815)

Most published data on radiotherapy for acromegaly comes from Gamma Knife machines; this single-center Brazilian cohort instead tracked 48 patients treated with LINAC-based stereotactic radiotherapy between 2008 and 2024, comparing single-dose (radiosurgery) against fractionated treatment. Tumor control was achieved in 48 of 49 lesions (98%), with a mean progression-free interval of over seven years. Fractionated treatment trended toward a longer time to hormonal cure than single-dose radiosurgery, though the difference wasn't statistically significant, and using octreotide or cabergoline alongside radiotherapy didn't change outcomes either way. It's a modest, single-center dataset, but it adds real-world evidence that LINAC systems — more widely available than Gamma Knife units — are a reasonable option when surgery alone doesn't normalize hormone levels.

**Cost-effectiveness of pasireotide long-acting release in acromegaly: a systematic literature review and methodology assessment**
Gilis-Januszewska A, Bronikowska M, Binowski G, et al. *Journal of Medical Economics* (2026)
[PMID: 41518621](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41518621/) | [DOI: 10.1080/13696998.2025.2609506](https://doi.org/10.1080/13696998.2025.2609506)

Pasireotide LAR and pegvisomant are the two main second-line options once first-generation somatostatin analogs stop working, but which is the better economic bet has been murky. This review combed six published cost-effectiveness studies and found the field split: two studies favored pegvisomant, but the single study judged most methodologically rigorous concluded pasireotide LAR was the more cost-effective choice. The authors' real takeaway is about the evidence itself — existing cost models vary so much in their assumptions and data sources that confident head-to-head conclusions aren't yet possible. Useful context given both drugs feature in the case report below.

**Beyond biochemical control: headache resolution with pasireotide in a patient with acromegaly and residual tumor**
Ekhzaimy AA, Alghamdi A, Alghamlas S, et al. *JCEM Case Reports* (2026)
[PMID: 42394805](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42394805/) | [DOI: 10.1210/jcemcr/luag063](https://doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag063)

A case report worth flagging for clinicians managing tough, surgically inoperable disease: a 40-year-old woman with a growth hormone- and prolactin-co-secreting tumor invading both cavernous sinuses had persistent high IGF-1 and severe headaches despite three years of surgery, radiotherapy, octreotide, cabergoline, and pegvisomant. Switching to pasireotide LAR normalized her IGF-1 and completely resolved the headaches. It's one patient, not a trial, but it adds to a growing pile of real-world reports that pasireotide can help when anatomy rules out further surgery and other drugs have plateaued.

## Part B — Rare Disease Industry News

**Can ultrasound unlock the power of gene therapy? SonoThera makes a bold claim in Duchenne muscular dystrophy**
STAT News, July 17, 2026 — [statnews.com/2026/07/17/sonathera-ultrasound-duchenne-muscular-dystrophy-treatment](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/17/sonathera-ultrasound-duchenne-muscular-dystrophy-treatment/)

SonoThera, a startup that recently raised $125 million, says it can use ultrasound to guide gene therapy into muscle tissue without the viral vectors that dominate the field today. In monkeys, the company reported the approach delivered enough full-length dystrophin to increase calf muscle size by 290% — nearly four times the dystrophin levels seen in healthy humans, according to the company's own data. Outside researchers are openly skeptical: ultrasound-guided delivery is an old idea that's generally been considered too weak for this kind of job, and SonoThera has no human data yet. *Why it matters for rare disease developers:* if the biology holds up in people, ultrasound delivery could sidestep some of the manufacturing bottlenecks and immune-response problems that have plagued viral gene therapy for muscular dystrophies — but extraordinary preclinical claims warrant proportionally extraordinary clinical evidence before the field gets ahead of itself.

**Amylyx nears pivotal readout for a drug targeting a rare endocrine disorder**
STAT News (Adam's Biotech Scorecard), July 16, 2026 — [statnews.com/2026/07/16/amylyx-bph-avexitide-biogen-diranersen-agios](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/16/amylyx-bph-avexitide-biogen-diranersen-agios/)

Amylyx Pharmaceuticals — the company that voluntarily pulled its ALS drug Relyvrio off the market two years ago after a confirmatory trial failed — is due to read out pivotal data later this quarter for avexitide, aimed at severe, uncontrolled hypoglycemia that some patients develop after bariatric (stomach-reducing) surgery. It's described as a pivotal moment that could support an approval filing. *Why it matters for rare disease developers:* Amylyx's Relyvrio withdrawal was widely seen as a rare instance of a biotech doing the right thing at real financial cost; how avexitide reads out will shape whether investors treat that decision as a one-off or as a marker of the company's underlying execution.

## What to watch

The throughline this week is measurement and evidence quality: a new AI tool that can out-diagnose specialists from a photo of a hand, a radiotherapy cohort quietly building the real-world case for more widely available LINAC machines, and a cost-effectiveness review that essentially concludes the health-economics literature on pasireotide isn't rigorous enough to trust yet. On the industry side, SonoThera's monkey data is the kind of result that either rewrites the gene-therapy delivery playbook or doesn't hold up to scrutiny — worth watching for human data before drawing conclusions. And keep an eye on Amylyx's pivotal readout this quarter: after voluntarily pulling Relyvrio, a positive result for avexitide would be a meaningful vote of confidence in the company's judgment, with implications for how much benefit of the doubt rare disease drug developers get after a setback.
